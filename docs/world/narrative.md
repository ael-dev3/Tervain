# Narrative foundation and first playable dispute

> Status: PROPOSED original story material, not approved canon or a commitment to launch scope. The accepted direction is serious single-player high fantasy, meaningful faction interactions, and a world understandable independently of Warpkeep. Every plot, person, ending, and historical interpretation below is a working proposal. See [vision](../vision.md), [setting](setting.md), and [factions](factions.md).

The Templars' Hegemony affiliation is confirmed by the owner (A21). The proposed local campaign remains away from the main Hegemony–Core–Ousters war; affiliation alone does not settle the order's response to each local dispute or adopt the narrative details below as canon.

## Narrative promise

The player earns a place in a landscape whose inhabitants already have relationships, obligations, and unresolved arguments. Exploration changes what the player knows; knowledge changes which actions become credible; actions change who can live, work, travel, or exercise authority in a place.

The proposed central question is: **Who has the right to change a shared inheritance when preserving it unchanged would also cause harm?** The Accord of the Wells makes that question concrete through public water, sanctuary, maintenance, and emergency powers. Its meaning can be tested in a village repair before the story expands to regional politics.

The wider dramatic pressure is a Marcher coalition extending emergency rule across Alder Basin while League towns defend their charters and the Templars dispute their own role. This produces occupied crossings, relief delivered under coercive terms, sanctuary disputes, armed resistance, and opportunities to demand enforceable limits. Water is Bellwether's immediate problem; political authority, livelihood, faith, violence, and belonging sustain the wider campaign. The north and desert pursue their own interests rather than becoming automatic extensions of this struggle.

The player should have a personal reason to seek livelihood and standing, not automatic status as a chosen savior. A flexible proposed opening is arrival with a work caravan delayed by a damaged crossing. The player needs passage and payment. Their background can explain basic capabilities without deciding ancestry, religion, or final allegiance. Exact background options remain open pending production scope.

## Dramatic rules

- Give each major dispute a material basis before a mythic interpretation: blocked water, unsafe work, lost grazing, a missing witness, or an unpaid obligation.
- Let people act on incomplete knowledge. Incorrect testimony should have a cause such as limited access, fear, self-interest, or honest misunderstanding.
- Make moral cost legible before irreversible action. Hidden discoveries can change an assessment; a surprise consequence should still follow from evidence in the world.
- Permit competent cooperation. A serious setting needs generosity, humor, and reliable people alongside corruption and rivalry.
- Avoid making every negotiated result optimal. Some needs cannot all be met within the available time, labor, or physical limits.
- Keep supernatural truths local enough to investigate. The player need not solve the universe's origin to decide what to do at a spring.
- Treat killing a political opponent as an event with successors and witnesses, not as an automatic solution to their constituency's needs.

## Proposed three-act campaign structure

This is the aspirational story shape. Only the Bellwether dispute is the initial production target; later acts must survive scope review before becoming a content commitment.

| Act | Player's widening role | Main dramatic work | What may remain unresolved |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| I — A Broken Course | Outsider becomes a useful and disputed participant | Stabilize Bellwether's damaged waterway and allocate its reduced flow; discover how the Accord is interpreted locally | Responsibility is distributed; one successful repair does not settle ownership |
| II — The Price of Keeping Faith | Trusted traveler or partisan negotiates between communities | Confront the Marcher emergency mandate, League resistance, and divided sanctuaries; follow debts and witness records into independent northern and southern interests | Protection and coercion can coexist; no institution holds a complete account of the past |
| III — The Gathering of Witnesses | A person with earned standing can influence an enforceable regional settlement | Contest whether emergency rule becomes permanent, who commands the crossings, which communities retain authority, and who can enforce the resulting duties | A workable peace can leave excluded groups, ecological losses, or debts that the epilogue acknowledges |

The campaign's escalation comes from linked systems and accumulated authority. A regional assembly can be as decisive as a battle if earlier choices determine who can reach it, whose evidence survives, and which parties can carry out its decision. Combat remains meaningful where violence blocks paths or threatens people, but the final answer need not be a single boss encounter.

Proposed broad conclusions include a reformed Accord with local accountability; stronger estate coordination with enforceable public duties; a federation of local agreements with reduced central authority; or an emergency settlement that preserves immediate survival while leaving deep divisions. These are not equally good in every world state. Their credibility depends on actual support, supplies, and previous conduct. There is no mandatory final betrayal by the Templars or predetermined machine reveal.

## Bellwether Vale: “The Dry Bell”

The working slice quest begins after late-thaw flooding partially breaks the old sluice upstream of Rillford and deposits sediment in the spring's channels. The village sounds its drought warning bell because usable flow has fallen: household channels and the communal mill lack water, quarry work becomes unsafe and unreliable, and the shrine wetland receives irregular flow. A flood creating a later shortage should be evident in sediment, damaged works, and blocked channels. Determining long-term responsibility is related to immediate relief but distinct.

### Proposed local cast

| Person | Position and practical need | What they know | What they misunderstand or conceal |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Mara Venn** | Rillford elected reeve; needs planting water and a credible labor plan | Households have already contributed more than the public ledger records | Authorized an emergency side diversion without all witnesses; believes its small size makes it irrelevant |
| **Sister Edda Sorn** | Templar waterkeeper; needs the spring and its wetland to survive | Older repair notes specify a flow pattern omitted from later copies | Deferred an inspection and underestimated the flood risk; defends the order before acknowledging its failure |
| **Darin Kest** | Quarry foreman; needs safe work and wages for his crew | A side seep increased after a recent cut; workers reported it | Kept a short extension working after permission expired; does not know whether it undermined the sluice |
| **Ila Rusk** | Rimeward stoneworker doing seasonal maintenance; needs rescue from an unsafe route, payment, and passage home | Can distinguish fresh fractures from old settlement and knows a safe gate procedure | Initially assumes officials will ignore an outsider; her technical knowledge does not establish every party's motives |

These four are the slice's principal quest actors. Stable implementation identifiers are `rillford_reeve`, `spring_steward`, `quarry_foreman`, and `maintenance_worker`; the proposed personal names can change without breaking content. Two optional secondary voices are estate steward **Oren Halvek**, who conceals how little money remains in the repair reserve, and Ash Witness recorder **Sel Anrit**, who mistakes a pattern of institutional failures for proof of inevitable collapse. They need no additional slice quest chains and may appear initially through a letter or a short ambient conversation.

A Saltward channelwright's annotated measuring kit in the village provides a seventh perspective without requiring another full quest-giver. Its instructions help measure safe operating levels and alternating service periods after stabilization. It supports the existing repair and allocation paths, not an additional resolution that creates more water.

### Facts, claims, and unresolved questions

The proposed author-facing baseline is a damaging late-thaw flood and sediment acting on a neglected structure. A town diversion, quarry seepage through fractured support, and deferred inspection compound the shortage. No single publicly blamed group explains all the evidence. There is no required saboteur hiding behind the dispute. Closing one diversion cannot restore the former supply, and forcing the damaged gate risks further failure.

Before implementation, a designer must fix the hydraulic layout and evidence sequence together. Every witness claim must be consistent with that model. Exact quantities and damage thresholds remain design tasks; the game should not present unspecified details as simulated facts. The canonical slice behavior and quest phases live in [quests and consequences](../design/quests-and-consequences.md).

Observable evidence can include differently weathered fractures, a diversion gate with fresh tool marks, missed inspection entries, water staining above normal levels, abandoned fish nests, and separate copies of the maintenance ledger. Spiritual investigation may reveal distress at the spring without identifying who signed an unlawful work order. No ability should replace every form of inquiry.

### Player sequence

1. **Encounter the consequences.** Hear the warning bell, reach the dry village channel, or meet quarry workers. Each entry leads into the same dispute.
2. **Walk the system.** Visit the fields, quarry, sluice, and shrine. Physical observation should challenge the first confident explanation the player hears.
3. **Secure cooperation.** Stabilize the support using an available brace and tool plus the worker's procedure or careful inspection. The two supporting encounters rescue Ila and recover a rotation ledger through permission or trespass. Neither is mandatory.
4. **Choose an allocation.** Present a workable proposal or exercise emergency control. Warn affected people when harm is foreseeable; refusal and dissent remain visible.
5. **See the result.** Show workers returning or leaving, changed access, repaired or redirected flow, altered shrine activity, and revised obligations. The slice ends with a concrete aftermath and a reason to travel onward.

### Proposed allocation outcomes

| Allocation after safe stabilization | Immediate benefit | Cost or dependency | Political consequence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Prioritize Rillford | Household supply improves and the mill resumes restricted work | Quarry work pauses; wages and the estate delivery suffer | Village leaders gain confidence; workers demand support for lost income |
| Prioritize the quarry | Limited quarry work and an important delivery resume | Mill remains closed; households continue rationing above the safe minimum | Estate contract interests benefit; village leaders challenge the allocation |
| Establish witnessed rotation | Both users receive alternating periods of service | Neither meets full demand; supervision and inconvenient shifts require real labor | Creates a shared obligation that needs continuing consent and accountability |

All normal outcomes preserve minimum drinking and habitat flow. Stabilization repairs gate control without creating extra water. An optional spring rite can make work safer; it cannot remove scarcity. Forcing the mechanism before stabilization jams it and adds a recoverable repair step. Rotation is no perfect option: limited hours, night shifts, and supervision create continuing costs.

Failure or withdrawal should leave a comprehensible state, such as temporary rationing and an imposed repair, rather than immediately destroying the valley. Major NPC deaths, lost evidence, and refusal to cooperate should route into authored fallback states where feasible. Scope those cases explicitly instead of promising universal reactivity.

## Consequences and future hooks

Record concrete outcomes: which intervention was used, who paid, whose testimony was recognized, whether threatened residents were warned, which promises remain outstanding, and who controls inspection access. Local trust should respond to witnessed behavior. Distant factions learn through travelers, letters, and institutions; they do not instantly know every private act.

The slice aftermath can show a turning mill beside an idle quarry, working stone crews with continued village rationing, or a posted rotation with exhausted night workers. Later campaign epilogues can expand those consequences into migration, debt, and changed authority. Work routines, dialogue, access, and scenery should support journal text. See [quests and consequences](../design/quests-and-consequences.md).

Possible later threads include an upstream assembly contesting downstream water claims, an oasis court holding an original treaty copy, and an estate asking for aid it previously refused to provide. None requires distant regions to be built for the first slice. The immediate ending should resolve the valley's playable dispute while leaving the player's earned relationships useful.

The [vertical-slice plan](../production/vertical-slice.md) defines the delivery boundary. This document provides dramatic intent, evidence constraints, and reusable relationships; it is not a substitute for a scene inventory, dialogue budget, or implemented quest-state specification.
