# Tervain: setting foundation

> Status: PROPOSED world design, not approved canon. The accepted direction is an original, serious, single-player high-fantasy game for Steam, centered on exploration and diverse factions around an ecological religious order. Tervain is the approved game title. Every place, historical event, custom, and supernatural rule below is a working proposal. See [vision](../vision.md) and [decisions](../decisions.md).

## The land and its central question

Tervain is the working name for the region in which the game takes place; whether it denotes a continent, a broad cultural area, or a country remains open. Its people inherit an agreement that made settlement possible in a landscape whose water, forests, and spiritual life cannot be separated. They disagree over who may interpret that agreement when survival requires changing the land.

The player encounters inhabited countryside: charcoal burners buying bread, seasonal workers sleeping above a stable, shrines repaired with mismatched stone, patrols asking for toll papers, and families arguing about whether their children should leave. Great institutions become understandable through these lives. Wilderness offers danger and discovery, but settlements have routes, resources, and histories that explain their presence.

The setting shares Warpkeep's wider world, and the Templars are part of the Hegemony (owner clarification A21). The Hegemony–Core–Ousters main conflict remains outside this local story. No prior knowledge of Warpkeep is required, and no chapter depends on an invasion by those powers, a hidden machine intelligence, or a revelation that local magic is technology. The formal hierarchy, timeline, and doctrine beyond that confirmed affiliation remain proposed or open.

## Three regional identities

These are proposed world-building territories, not a commitment to three fully produced launch regions. The first playable slice occupies one small valley.

| Region | Environment and everyday economy | Local political question | Exploration character |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Alder Basin | Temperate woodland, braided rivers, orchards, wetlands, grain terraces; timber, food, river trade, stone | Who may alter inherited rights to land and water? | Roads through farms, wooded ridges, mill channels, old bridges, overgrown boundary shrines |
| Rimeward Heights | Cold uplands, high pastures, sheltered pine valleys; wool, iron, salt meat, seasonal labor | Who speaks for a watershed that begins on clan land and supports strangers below? | Mountain passes, avalanche shelters, mining galleries, thaw lakes, ancestral assembly grounds |
| Saltward Expanse | Dry basins, rocky escarpments, salt flats and oasis chains; caravan trade, dates, glass, well craft | Can shared access survive when water becomes both a necessity and a political weapon? | Caravan tracks, cistern steps, shade courts, ruined channels, wind-cut sanctuaries |

The north and south possess their own political centers. They are neither distant quest appendices nor culturally uniform supporters of a central war. Their leaders can cooperate over one river agreement and refuse involvement in an unrelated basin dispute. Regional styles should express climate and available materials without reducing a culture to a single real-world analogue.

### Alder Basin

The basin supplies much of the region's grain. Its towns depend on upstream forests holding soil and regulating runoff. Marcher estates supply roads and armed protection; Hearth League communities supply labor, carts, and taxes. Templar sanctuaries protect sensitive springs and teach waterkeeping, but their restrictions can prevent a village from opening desperately needed fields. A prosperous downstream town may present its dependence as a sacred right while paying very little toward upstream stewardship.

### Rimeward Heights

Clans maintain difficult passes and enforce grazing agreements. Leadership derives from negotiated obligations among households rather than a single northern king. A mine owner, herding family, sanctuary, and forge community can belong to the same clan and want different outcomes. Winter stores and access to lower pastures make the north economically connected to the basin despite its political independence.

### Saltward Expanse

Oasis states organize through the Salt Concord. Public wells, private reservoirs, caravan guarantees, and emergency shelter rights make law visible in daily life. The Concord's engineers dispute the basin assumption that leaving nature untouched is inherently virtuous: their ancestors built the channels that keep their communities alive. Some sanctuaries accept this argument; others fear that copying oasis methods upstream will harm the whole river system.

## The central struggle: emergency protection becoming permanent rule

The proposed campaign's main political conflict is concentrated in **Alder Basin**. After damaged roads, failed harvest arrangements, and neglected works expose the weakness of divided authority, a coalition within the Marcher Houses claims a temporary mandate to secure crossings, requisition supplies, direct repairs, and collect emergency levies. Its patrols restore some roads and prevent some violence. They also occupy tollhouses, displace elected officials, and treat expired permissions as continuing powers.

League towns resist the loss of their charters. Some refuse the new levy, hide supplies, or organize armed escorts; others accept a garrison because their own council cannot keep the road safe. Houses outside the coalition fear both disorder and a rival's growing army. The Templars divide over whether to certify the mandate, condition it on public duties, or shelter those resisting it. Sanctuary gates, bridges, grain stores, and witness records become strategically important places.

The conflict should be visible during travel: contested checkpoints, a confiscated wagon, a working bridge repaired with compulsory labor, a shrine sheltering displaced households, and a town whose gates close after a confrontation. It can escalate into raids and local fighting without requiring a continent-wide war or a nonhuman enemy species. An original high-fantasy conflict can involve human institutions, dangerous creatures, and sacred places whose protection carries real force.

The player's larger question is whether emergency authority becomes lasting domination, can be bound to enforceable duties, or should give way to a different settlement. No faction automatically owns the humane answer. Who receives protection, whose testimony counts, and who can afford resistance depend on local conduct.

Rimeward assemblies and the Salt Concord remain independent. They bargain over passes, food, maintenance, debt, and transit rather than automatically joining either side. An upland clan can sell metal to a house while sheltering League refugees; an oasis court can deny a military loan while enforcing an unpopular caravan contract. Their stories must make sense on their own.

This is a campaign proposal, not added slice scope. Bellwether introduces it through the quarry contract, delayed relief, and disputed obligations. The first playable does not require a military occupation system, faction army simulation, or a second regional quest line.

## The Accord of the Wells

The Accord is a founding compact between communities and institutions, not a spell that mechanically enforces peace. It arose after a period of feuding in which dams, burned watersheds, and broken roads made nominal military victories unlivable.

Its proposed enduring obligations are:

1. A traveler may draw enough public water to survive; sale, irrigation, and industry remain governed locally.
2. Permanent changes to a watercourse require recorded testimony from affected residents, downstream users, and a recognized waterkeeper. Waterkeepers may be Templars or locally trained stewards.
3. Communities benefiting from shared works owe maintenance labor, material, or a negotiated payment.
4. A sanctuary may shelter the displaced temporarily; sanctuary does not erase obligations to people harmed by the sheltered person.
5. Emergency diversions expire unless reviewed. Scarcity does not automatically convert a temporary measure into permanent ownership.

Enforcement relies on witnesses, copies of agreements, access to skilled labor, trade pressure, and force. Surviving records contradict one another. Some communities never signed the original compact but adopted selected practices later. The Templars preserve important archives without owning the entire legal tradition.

This produces practical conflicts: a lord presents a genuine grant that omits tenants; a town fulfills its payment obligation but upstream work never happens; an old river changes course and leaves a treaty describing a landscape that no longer exists. Most quests should expose such concrete problems before invoking destiny.

## Faith, magic, and uncertainty

The proposed shared religious premise is that places can retain the consequences of repeated living acts. A tended spring, burial ground, or ancient orchard can develop a presence that people recognize through ritual. Cultures disagree on whether these presences are persons, ancestors, aspects of divinity, or properties of the world.

Magic is real in the fiction. Its mechanisms remain partly uncertain to its practitioners. A rite may clarify foul water, encourage a damaged root to bind soil, or preserve a dying person's last intelligible memory. It cannot freely create food, replace all medicine, restore every dead person, or erase political consequences. A successful intervention can consume stored materials, require trained cooperation, exhaust a place, or create an obligation elsewhere. Mechanical costs still require prototyping; these examples are narrative boundaries.

Different disciplines should be credible. A Saltward channelwright can know something a Templar does not; a clan funerary keeper may understand a local presence that ignores imported rites. Supernatural success does not prove every claim made by a religion's hierarchy. Ecological health also does not equal human comfort: a flourishing wetland can flood a village that settled in the wrong place.

## Proposed chronology

Dates use years since the Accord to avoid prematurely fixing a cosmology or calendar.

| Date | Proposed event | Trace visible to the player |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Before year 0 | Generations of local wars damage shared water and road systems | Abandoned channels, fortified wells, contradictory heroic monuments |
| Year 0 | Multiple communities establish the first Accord of the Wells | Original witness stones and later copies with disputed additions |
| Years 35–80 | Waterkeeping schools and sanctuaries expand; trade towns flourish | Similar practical works built in distinct regional styles |
| Years 110–140 | Estates consolidate; towns purchase privileges; maintenance debts accumulate | Toll charters, displaced farm boundaries, patched infrastructure |
| Year 169 | A severe drought leads to emergency water grants | Temporary arrangements treated as acquired rights |
| Year 174, present | A hard winter and late-thaw flooding expose old failures | Damaged crossings, shortages, arguments over whose warning was ignored |

This is a framework rather than five years of uniform disaster. People still celebrate weddings, make profitable trades, and enjoy summer markets. A world that functions gives its threatened institutions meaning.

## Bellwether Vale: the first close view

The proposed playable valley contains Rillford village, a ford and footbridge, an old sluice, a spring shrine and wetland, a small quarry, fields, and wooded paths. Routes should let the player understand their physical relationship by walking them. Water leaves the shrine's upland catchment, feeds a managed channel and adjacent wetland, then passes village holdings and the ford. The quarry intersects a side seep; its work and the main channel affect each other without occupying the same location.

A late-thaw flood damaged the sluice and deposited sediment in spring channels, producing a shortage of usable water after the flood. Rillford needs household supply and its communal mill restored; irrigation and planting create longer-term pressure. Quarry workers need safe, adequate water for operations and wages. Shrine keepers protect minimum habitat flow and the spring's presence. A town diversion, quarry seepage through fractured support, and deferred inspection compound the damage. Small decisions over access, materials, and testimony introduce the larger world without a single required saboteur.

The valley should include mundane evidence of interdependence: quarry stone in shrine steps, temple remedies in workers' houses, clan wool in a merchant's store, and a Saltward measuring tool. These connections make later relationships plausible before those regions exist as playable spaces. The slice's three allocation outcomes favor village use, quarry work, or a costly rotation while preserving basic drinking and habitat thresholds.
